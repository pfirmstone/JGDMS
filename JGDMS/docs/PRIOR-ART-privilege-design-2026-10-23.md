# Prior art leaves three JVM mechanisms unclaimed

Across roughly sixty primary sources (OpenJDK JEPs and review threads, the Elasticsearch entitlements source tree, the OSGi R8 specification, IBM's access-rights analysis papers, Trishul, IBAC, Phosphor, Laminar, Microsoft's CAS retirement notes, Jini's httpmd design, and the canonical-encoding RFCs), **no precedent was found for any of the three mechanisms as specified**: nobody has made every `AccessController.doPrivileged` intersect the caller's authority with a policy-granted per-class domain, nobody has stamped deserialized objects with a provenance domain that a stack walk collects from frame receivers, and nobody has used a digest of a canonically encoded schema as a security gate or an authorization-policy key. Each mechanism nonetheless has near-misses that a novelty claim must name and distinguish. For bounded elevation the nearest precedent is **ZTD_JAVA (Purdue/Google, 2023)**, which observes runs, infers per-dependency least-privilege policy and intersects every frame on the stack with a measured **DaCapo median overhead of −0.03% against 21.06% for the Java SecurityManager** ([arXiv 2310.14117](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.14117)), but it keys on dependency namespaces and never touches `doPrivileged`; the only shipped bounding of `doPrivileged`, JEP 140, let the privileged code name its own ceiling rather than having policy name it ([JEP 140](https://openjdk.org/jeps/140)). For receiver provenance the two citations to distinguish are **Trishul (VU Amsterdam IR-CS-45)**, where a method's initial context taint includes the taint of `this` and policies match on `thisTaint` ([Trishul](https://www.srijith.net/publications/techreports/Trishul-IR-CS-45.pdf)), and **IBAC (Pistoia/Banerjee/Naumann, IEEE S&P 2007)**, which labels values with permission sets and checks them at the same `test` points as stack inspection, including through privileged blocks ([sp07.pdf](https://software.imdea.org/~ab/Publications/sp07.pdf)); both paid 52–167% for full propagation and neither reused `ProtectionDomain` as the carrier. The decisive design lesson comes from .NET: Microsoft removed `Deny` in .NET Framework 4 because "A Deny action could be easily overridden by an Assert action" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/dotnet/netframework-4.0/dd233103(v=vs.100))), and a receiver-derived provenance domain that sits below a `doPrivileged` frame is masked in exactly the same way, so provenance collection must continue past privileged frames, which is the choice IBAC made. For digest identities, the field's consensus is to hash transport bytes rather than a canonical re-encoding, every schema-fingerprint scheme explicitly disclaims security (Avro: fingerprints "are not meant to provide any security guarantees" ([Avro 1.11.1](https://avro.apache.org/docs/1.11.1/specification/))), and every canonical form that has been hashed in anger (CMS BER/DER, Bitcoin pre-BIP 66, XML C14N, CBOR 7049→8949, JCS `-0`) has produced a "verifier hashes form A, consumer acts on form B" bug whose fix is always to reject non-distinguished input before hashing and to act on the very bytes that were hashed.

## Bounded elevation: every piece exists, the combination does not

The question for mechanism (1) is whether anyone has removed unbounded elevation from the JVM by forcing every `doPrivileged` through a policy-bounded path keyed on the calling class. The answer, as far as the available searches establish, is no. Searches of security-dev and jdk-dev for a proposal to remove unbounded `doPrivileged` returned nothing relevant, and no OpenJDK JEP, mailing-list proposal, OSGi specification or paper reached proposes making every `doPrivileged` policy-bounded by caller identity. The three closest moves in the historical record are JEP 140, which added an optional bounded form beside the unbounded ones and left them in place; Elasticsearch's entitlements, which removed `doPrivileged` by abandoning stack intersection altogether; and JEP 486, which removed elevation by removing enforcement, so that in JDK 24 "the six AccessController::doPrivileged methods execute the given action immediately" ([JEP 486](https://openjdk.org/jeps/486)). The design under comparison appears novel in combining mandatory bounding of every elevation, a per-class policy key, and recorded rather than hand-written policy; each piece individually has a precedent, which is where the distinctions have to be drawn.

**JEP 140 (JDK 8, 2013) is the only shipped bounding of `doPrivileged`, and it bounds in the wrong direction for this design.** Its limited overloads take a `Permission[]` argument; for a matching check the walk "would stop walking the access-control context (acc) stack at that doPrivileged() invocation and succeed," while a non-matching check falls back to the class's normal privileges and continues "walking the full acc stack as if the limited doPrivileged() had not been invoked" ([JEP 140](https://openjdk.org/jeps/140)). The ceiling is therefore chosen by the privileged code at each call site, not by an external policy keyed on the class; the design under comparison replaces this *self-bounded* form with a *policy-bounded* one, and no source describes anyone building the latter on top of the former. The JEP contains no discussion of risks, performance or known problems, its only identified in-JDK consumer is JEP 184's HTTP `URLPermission` bootstrap case ([JEP 184](https://openjdk.org/jeps/184)), and the code-review thread shows a single reviewer asking for "some summary on how they will be used in determining limited privilege scope" with no performance discussion ([security-dev, May 2013](https://mail.openjdk.org/pipermail/security-dev/2013-May/007632.html)). The reasons it stayed little used have no primary-source answer; the evidence supports only the inference that it was a narrow enabler for JEP 184, not a least-privilege program.

**Elasticsearch entitlements are the nearest shipped post-SecurityManager system, and they are the structural opposite of stack intersection.** Policy is keyed on the Java module within a component (`PolicyScope(ComponentKind kind, String componentName, String moduleName)`), cached per `java.lang.Module` object (`computeIfAbsent(requestingClass.getModule(), ...)`), and non-modular plugins collapse into the catch-all `ALL-UNNAMED` scope ([PolicyManager.java](https://github.com/elastic/elasticsearch/blob/main/libs/entitlement/src/main/java/org/elasticsearch/entitlement/runtime/policy/PolicyManager.java); [README](https://github.com/elastic/elasticsearch/blob/main/libs/entitlement/README.md)). Checks are chokepoints that ASM instrumentation injects at the start of JDK methods, and the caller is identified as "the first untrusted frame": a `StackWalker` that filters out the entitlements module, skips one frame, and takes the first remaining one ([PolicyCheckerImpl.java](https://github.com/elastic/elasticsearch/blob/main/libs/entitlement/src/main/java/org/elasticsearch/entitlement/runtime/policy/PolicyCheckerImpl.java)). Everything in the boot layer's system modules is trivially allowed, with exactly two exceptions, `java.desktop` and `java.xml`, which are deliberately not trusted ([PolicyManager.java](https://github.com/elastic/elasticsearch/blob/main/libs/entitlement/src/main/java/org/elasticsearch/entitlement/runtime/policy/PolicyManager.java)); the rationale for choosing those two was not found in any reachable source, but their exclusion is the only evidence that the authors considered JDK deputies dangerous at all. Because exactly one frame pays, there is no caller authority to intersect and hence no `doPrivileged` analogue; the README says plainly that code which under the SecurityManager used `doPrivileged` "might need an Entitlement now to run correctly" and that "the two models do not map one-to-one" ([README](https://github.com/elastic/elasticsearch/blob/main/libs/entitlement/README.md)). Policy is hand-written YAML per plugin and Java code for the server, with an emergency base64 JVM-option patch that "is intended to be used **only** for emergencies" and cannot remove an entitlement ([README](https://github.com/elastic/elasticsearch/blob/main/libs/entitlement/README.md)); no inferred or recorded policy generator is described anywhere. The practical cost of hand-written module-grained policy is visible in the field: LDAP/Active Directory authentication broke across 8.16.x, 8.17.x below 8.17.9, 8.18.x below 8.18.7 and 9.0.x below 9.0.4 because the x-pack-core policy omitted `outbound_network` for the transitive `unboundid.ldapsdk` module ([Search Guard blog, secondary](https://search-guard.com/blog/ldap-authentication-broken-elasticsearch-8-18-entitlements-bug/)); observability agents attaching to the JVM fail with "component [(unknown)], module [ALL-UNNAMED]" ([Elastic Discuss #380418](https://discuss.elastic.co/t/es-security-entitlements-v8-18-onwards/380418)); a classic plugin on 9.0.1 could not express a grant to read another jar's manifest ([Discuss #385137](https://discuss.elastic.co/t/elasticsearch-classic-plugin-problem-with-entitlements/385137)); and an 8.19.10 node refused to boot because the hard-coded forbidden-path rule for `/etc/elasticsearch` could not be overridden even by the emergency patch ([Discuss #384627](https://discuss.elastic.co/t/entitlements-and-tmpdir/384627)). No performance figure for entitlements appears in the README, the docs, or any Discuss thread reached. The lesson for a per-class key, which is finer than any shipped grain, is that it multiplies this class of "missing grant" regression unless policy is recorded rather than written, which is precisely the design's mitigation and ZTD_JAVA's method.

**ZTD_JAVA is the closest precedent for "observe runs, infer policy, intersect all frames," and the only system in the set with a published cost.** Its policies are keyed per dependency namespace (Maven groupId plus artifactId prefix) and "apply to all classes within the dependency"; enforcement is full-stack intersection ("An operation is authorized if all dependencies on the call stack are authorized"); and policies are generated by observation, with the direct caller receiving coarse and fine permissions and other frames receiving fine-grained "transitive" permissions so a dependency may act through a directly permitted one ([arXiv 2310.14117](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.14117)). Chokepoints are the `FileInputStream`/`FileOutputStream` constructors, `Socket.connect()` and `ProcessBuilder.start()`. Measured overhead: **file read +196.7% in a microbenchmark; DaCapo median −0.03% versus 21.06% for the Java SecurityManager; Eclipse 2.91% versus 201.04% for the SecurityManager**; the paper also asserts the SecurityManager cost "up to 200%" and counts 28 permission types over 100+ operations ([arXiv 2310.14117](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.14117)). Its stated limitations are the ones a recorded-policy design inherits: cross-thread and cross-process delegation unhandled, native-code bypass, unobserved behaviour producing false positives, namespace pollution, and reflection/callback indirection. ZTD_JAVA's "transitive" permission is its answer to the deputy question, and it is a policy construct rather than an elevation primitive; it does not bound or replace `doPrivileged`, because its model has none.

**The IBM access-rights line (Koved, Pistoia, Kershenbaum, OOPSLA 2002; Pistoia et al., ECOOP 2005) is the direct ancestor of "infer policy per class and validate `doPrivileged` placement."** The 2002 paper diagnoses the practice the design's audit SecurityManager replaces: the common strategy is to "execute the code, note authorization failures, allocate additional access rights, and test again," which "often leads to a violation of the 'Principle of Least Privilege'" ([Koved et al. 2002](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/ninghui/readings/AccessControl/koved_etal_oopsla02.pdf)). Its output is per class, computed by a context- and flow-sensitive interprocedural analysis over an access-rights invocation graph in which `doPrivileged` is a propagation boundary (permissions "do not propagate beyond the predecessor of the doPrivileged node"); it is path-insensitive and field-flow-insensitive, needs string-constant permission targets, and ran on javadoc (about 6,720 classes, about 170 s, about 128 MB) and ECPerf (about 21,291 classes, about 101 s), figures the researcher flags as the best reading of a garbled table ([Koved et al. 2002](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/ninghui/readings/AccessControl/koved_etal_oopsla02.pdf)). The 2005 work can "automatically identify which portions of trusted code should be made privileged," detect "unnecessary" and "redundant" privileged code, and check that variables used by privileged code are not "tainted" by client code ([IBM Research](https://research.ibm.com/publications/interprocedural-analysis-for-privileged-code-placement-and-tainted-variable-detection); [US 7,237,236](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7237236)). The documented limits of the static approach (unknown string targets, over-approximation) are exactly what a dynamic audit avoids at the price of coverage; Li and Le Thanh's later per-protection-domain generator produces "an over-approximation of the minimal policy" with no evaluation and observes that "A public tool that can automatically generating security policies for Java applications doesn't exist so far" ([arXiv 1307.2964](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.2964)).

Two further bodies of evidence bound what a per-class floor can and cannot fix. Security Explorations' 2012 catalogue documents the JDK-deputy failure mode empirically: **Oracle 31 issues of which 17 were full sandbox bypasses (29 of 31 fixed), IBM 17 issues of which 10 were full bypasses, Apple 2 of which 1**, with examples such as `sun.plugin.liveconnect.SecureInvocation.CallMethod`, which "provided a functionality to invoke arbitrary methods inside AccessController.doPrivileged() block," and an attack that "asserts one extra trusted stack frame" via `doPrivilegedWithCombiner()` with a system action object ([SE-2012-01 Devoxx slides](https://security-explorations.com/materials/se-2012-01-devoxx.pdf)). These were `doPrivileged` blocks whose inputs were attacker-controlled, Pistoia 2005's "tainted variables in privileged code"; a per-class floor tightens what such a deputy may do but does not by itself fix ProcessImpl or URL handlers whose arguments come from the caller, which is why the design treats them as the known residual. JEP 411 then records why the whole model was abandoned: "There is no way to have partial security," limiting "viral growth of permissions" requires "carefully using the java.security.AccessController API" whose complexity "is far beyond the interest of most developers," "the path of least resistance for application developers is often to grant AllPermission," and the access-control algorithm "often imposes an unacceptable performance penalty," with no number attached ([JEP 411](https://openjdk.org/jeps/411)). JEP 486 adds that "the permission scheme is so complex that the Security Manager has always been disabled by default" and that Derby, Ant, SpotBugs and Tomcat dropped support ([JEP 486](https://openjdk.org/jeps/486)).

The remaining systems are contrasts rather than precedents. OSGi ConditionalPermissionAdmin bounds a bundle by "Effective = ( Local ∩ System) ∪ Implied" over one `ProtectionDomain` per bundle with an ordered ALLOW/DENY table keyed on bundle location or signer, relies wholly on standard stack inspection so that condition implementers "should use the `AccessController` `doPrivileged`," and warns that naive evaluation of every condition for every bundle on the stack "would be prohibitively expensive" ([OSGi Core R8 §50](https://docs.osgi.org/specification/osgi.core/8.0.0/service.condpermadmin.html)); Knopflerfish's own documentation says the default "is to grant everyone AllPermission," that "the framework needs AllPermission," and that "turning on security only gives us a set-up hassle and a performance hit" without a number ([Knopflerfish 5.2.0](https://www.knopflerfish.org/releases/5.2.0/docs/osgi_with_security.html)); Equinox changed its fallback semantics between 3.4 and 3.5 so that "Once you add a single condition to the table then bundles must not be granted the PermissionAdmin default permissions" ([equinox-dev msg05503](https://www.eclipse.org/lists/equinox-dev/msg05503.html)); and Parrend and Frénot found permissions "extremely unconvenient to set and manage" with "a great cost in term of functionality, but also in term of performance," again unmeasured ([arXiv 0706.3812](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0706.3812)). Apache River's `DynamicPolicy` grants land on the class's **class loader**, not the class, and `Security.doPrivileged` is unbounded apart from preserving the Subject combiner ([River 3.0 Security](https://river.apache.org/release-doc/3.0.0/api/net/jini/security/Security.html); [policy package](https://river.apache.org/release-doc/3.0.0/api/net/jini/security/policy/package-summary.html)). GraalVM's sandbox is per polyglot context or engine, covers only ECMAScript 2022 and WebAssembly guests, and reports no overhead beyond "slower startup" ([GraalVM sandboxing](https://www.graalvm.org/latest/security-guide/sandboxing/)). Wallach and Felten note that stack inspection can let "highly-trusted code to operate with less than its full privileges" but describe no mechanism that forces it ([Wallach & Felten 1998](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/truselab/readings/javaStackInspection.pdf)).

## Receiver provenance: Trishul and IBAC are the citations to beat, and the Deny/Assert lesson governs the walk

The goal behind mechanism (2), making authority depend on where data came from rather than only on which code is on the stack, is well explored: Abadi and Fournet's history-based access control (NDSS 2003), IBAC (S&P 2007), Trishul (2007–08), Laminar (PLDI 2009, TOPLAS 2014) and Phosphor (OOPSLA 2014) all address it. The specific mechanism, stamping an object with a value-equal `ProtectionDomain`-like provenance domain at deserialization and having `getContext()` intersect the domains of the receivers that a `StackWalker` finds into an ordinary `AccessControlContext`, **was not found in any paper, JBS issue or mailing-list thread reached**. The searches that established this (all WebSearch, 2026-10-23) were: `LiveStackFrame getLocals getMonitors StackWalker java.base JDK-8137058`; `site:bugs.openjdk.org StackWalker receiver "this" object local variables security permission check`; `"object-based" OR "receiver" taint "access control" Java stack walk "AccessControlContext" data provenance ProtectionDomain object`; `"stack inspection" "data provenance" OR "object provenance" access control untrusted objects deserialization JVM`; and `"taint" "AccessController" OR "SecurityManager" "deserialized" objects trust label JVM runtime enforcement paper`. They returned JEP 259 implementation items, GNU Classpath and SableVM `VMAccessController` sources (class-domain-only walks), OWASP and CERT deserialization pages, Trishul, and two IBM patents on an actor-language security model, none proposing receiver-derived domains. The hotspot-runtime-dev and security-dev archives were searched only through general web search, not grepped directly, so an informal thread could still exist.

**Trishul is the only system found in which the receiver's provenance participates in a method's security context.** Built on a modified Kaffe JVM at VU Amsterdam (Nair, Simpson, Crispo, Tanenbaum; tech report IR-CS-45, latest references 2007), it gives every operand-stack value, local, parameter and return value a bitmap taint, keeps "an object taint stored in its own memory" per object and a member taint in shadow memory per field, and tracks implicit flows through a context taint held in an SSE register under the JIT. Crucially for this design, "Action patterns can match the taint of the calling object (`thisTaint`). The initial context taint of a method includes the taint of the `this` pointer for instance methods" ([Trishul IR-CS-45](https://www.srijith.net/publications/techreports/Trishul-IR-CS-45.pdf)). Trishul enforces rather than merely detects: method calls are hooked as actions and a policy engine returns orders (OK, Halt, Suppress, Exception, taint-modifying), so writing password-file-tainted data through a network-tainted `PrintStream` throws. Its costs are the costs of propagation: **CPU-bound prime sieve 685 ms to 1828 ms (+167%); I/O-bound file reader +1%; load-time analysis +12.9% with about 1951 bytes of metadata per method; a never-matched static policy about 2%; a never-matched dynamic policy 31,933 ms against a 4.5 ms baseline, described as the worst case** ([Trishul IR-CS-45](https://www.srijith.net/publications/techreports/Trishul-IR-CS-45.pdf)). Deserialization is not discussed and network data is not labelled by default. The distinction a novelty claim must draw is therefore precise: Trishul reads receiver provenance, but it obtains it by per-value propagation in a custom JVM with shadow memory per member, and it feeds a separate policy engine; it does not stamp at the object-graph boundary, does not collect from stack frames at check time, and does not reuse the JDK's `ProtectionDomain`/`AccessControlContext`/`Policy.implies` machinery. The researcher could not confirm Trishul's publication venue beyond the tech report.

**IBAC (Pistoia, Banerjee, Naumann, "Beyond Stack Inspection," IEEE S&P 2007) is the closest conceptual ancestor on the label side.** It holds that "every access-control policy α implicitly defines an information-flow policy ι," stores values as framed values `R[v]` where R "reflects the fact that v can be trusted at level at most R," attaches frames to references and heap fields, frames objects at allocation with their class's policy permissions, and turns existing `test` calls into checkpoints that check both the stack and the labels on values read by the guarded operation ([sp07.pdf](https://software.imdea.org/~ab/Publications/sp07.pdf)). Its permission-set labels live in the same lattice as `ProtectionDomain` permissions, which is why it is the nearest precedent for a provenance *domain*. Three differences separate the design from it: IBAC labels at every allocation and assignment, the design labels once at the deserialization boundary; IBAC collects labels from the values the guarded operation reads, the design collects from the receivers on the stack; and IBAC never reused `ProtectionDomain` as the carrier. IBAC's own limits are also documented: the formal model omits subclassing and dynamic dispatch so receiver-specific treatment is not discussed; the authors "implemented a subsystem of IBAC, which only enforces the rejection of integrity violations" from tainted variables in privilege-asserting code; there are no performance measurements, so no cost data exists for a per-value permission-set design; and capability escape (an escaped `FileOutputStream`) is an acknowledged gap ([sp07.pdf](https://software.imdea.org/~ab/Publications/sp07.pdf)). One further distinction matters for provenance-by-peer: both IBAC and Laminar label by allocation context or class policy, and Laminar explicitly requires network input to be "read by empty-label code and validated in a security method before use" ([Laminar TOPLAS 2014](https://www.cs.unc.edu/~porter/pubs/laminar-toplas.pdf)); such labels give deserialized objects the label of the deserializing code, not of the peer, and so cannot distinguish two peers deserialized by the same code path, which is exactly what a value-equal provenance domain is for.

**The .NET `Deny` removal is the pitfall that changes the design.** Microsoft's .NET Framework 4 security notes state that "Runtime support has been removed for enforcing the Deny, RequestMinimum, RequestOptional, and RequestRefuse permission requests," that "these requests were not well understood and presented the potential for security vulnerabilities when they were not used properly," and specifically that "A Deny action could be easily overridden by an Assert action. [...] The Assert prevented the Deny from being seen on the stack, making it ineffective" ([Microsoft Learn, .NET Framework 4 security changes](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/dotnet/netframework-4.0/dd233103(v=vs.100))). A receiver-derived provenance domain is a Deny: it subtracts authority. If the stack walk that collects provenance domains stops at a `doPrivileged` frame, as the ordinary authority walk does, then any provenance domain attached to a receiver below that frame is invisible, and the gadget path the mechanism exists for, an attacker-built object whose method is invoked inside trusted privileged code, is precisely the path on which it fails. The design must therefore either collect provenance domains only below the privileged frame, which reproduces the .NET weakness, or collect them across the whole stack regardless of privileged frames, which changes `doPrivileged` semantics. IBAC chose the latter for value labels: untrusted data passed through `grant`/privileged blocks is still caught because "the frame of a tainted field value is checked even when the stack walk stops at the privileged block" ([sp07.pdf](https://software.imdea.org/~ab/Publications/sp07.pdf)). The rest of the .NET record reinforces that no vendor saw data provenance as either the problem or the fix: CAS policy was retired as "complicated and restrictive" with guarantees that "do not apply to native applications," and .NET Core never implemented CAS, so the cross-compiled APIs "led to 'fail open' scenarios" in which `Assert` succeeds with no effect and Demands always succeed ([Microsoft Learn, .NET 5 breaking change](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/core/compatibility/core-libraries/5.0/code-access-security-apis-obsolete)).

The remaining lineages explain why nobody built this mechanism and what it inherits. HBAC is the mirror image of the design: current rights are "the intersection of the old current rights with the static rights of this code" and do not recover on return, with `Grant` and `Accept` as the amplification and restoration constructs; it was never integrated into the CLR ("we have yet to attempt a full development and integration"), carries no overhead numbers, and by its authors' description "keeps track of code execution but not data dependencies" ([Abadi & Fournet, NDSS 2003](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Access-Control-Based-on-Execution-History-Martin-Abadi.pdf)). Its documented pitfalls, rights that only decrease, callbacks still exploitable unless the callee stays on the stack, and "unhandled exceptions won't restore permissions" inside `Accept`, are pitfalls of stored state; the design recomputes per walk and so avoids them, at the price of seeing only objects that are receivers on the current stack. Banerjee and Naumann later showed HBAC "is itself a covert channel subject to nontrivial attacks," and in giving `self` a security level noted that "it is the class of the dynamically dispatched code that matters, not the class of the target object" ([Banerjee & Naumann](https://software.imdea.org/~ab/Publications/hbacsif.pdf)); the Abadi–Fournet patent US 7,203,833 (filed 23 Dec 2002, granted 10 Apr 2007, expired for fees) claims rights of code and execution units, with no visible claim attaching rights to data ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7203833)). Phosphor adds a tag field to every object and a shadow variable to every primitive, modifies every call site including reflective ones, and reports an **average 52% overhead on DaCapo 9.12-bach with integer tags and data-flow propagation**, with control-flow tracking at more than 10x ([Phosphor paper](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10112170); [README](https://github.com/Programming-Systems-Lab/phosphor)); it is a detection tool whose sinks "check their arguments for taint marks," and no Phosphor-based successor was found that denied an operation through the SecurityManager path. Laminar's per-object immutable labels in two header words cost **about 21% average on DaCapo/pseudojbb with dynamic barriers and about 10% with static barriers, with case studies of 5–56% in the TOPLAS version** and GradeSheet 12%, Battleship 84%, Calendar 14%, FreeCS at most 4% in the technical report ([Laminar TOPLAS](https://www.cs.unc.edu/~porter/pubs/laminar-toplas.pdf); [UT TR-08-45](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/ftp/techreports/tr08-45.pdf)); it never mentions stack inspection or `ProtectionDomain`. Jif offers authority "similar in expressiveness to the Java stack inspection mechanism, while avoiding the run-time overhead" and never mentions `SecurityManager` or `ProtectionDomain` ([Jif manual](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/jif/doc/jif-2.0.0/jif_programming.html)); the IFC lineage treats stack inspection as something to replace, so it had no reason to reuse a `ProtectionDomain` as a label carrier. Joe-E removes ambient authority so that "a reference to one is the capability to use it," describes provenance as arising transitively "when a trusted object returns another object," and expects Java's own mechanisms to be "better-suited to enforcing security on legacy code" ([Joe-E, NDSS 2010](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/met.pdf)); in ocap terms the provenance domain is a *negative* capability, a notion ocap systems do not need because an untrusted object simply has no authority, and the design targets exactly the legacy case Joe-E cedes to stack inspection. Wallach's formalisation has "Every frame on the call stack includes a reference to the class running in that frame" and claims security-passing style costs "in principal, just over one instruction per method invocation," with nothing attributing object identity to frames ([Wallach thesis, 1999](https://cs.yale.edu/homes/dsc5/papers/dwallach-thesis.pdf)). The earlier Deeds system (CCS 1998) kept per-program event histories keyed by SHA-1 of downloaded code at under 5% overhead (0.7% with one handler, 4.3% with ten, plus 0.4–5.6 s startup hashing) and tracked no data provenance ([Edjlali et al.](https://www.cse.buffalo.edu/cadi/papers/1998/1998_3.pdf)).

Two practical facts bound the implementation. First, the JDK exposes the primitive the mechanism needs only inside `java.base`: `LiveStackFrame` is declared package-private, its javadoc says it "is intended to be package-private or move to an internal package," its `getLocals()`, `getStack()` and `getMonitors()` are gated by `RuntimePermission("liveStackFrames")` under a SecurityManager that JEP 486 has permanently disabled, and the text never mentions the receiver ([LiveStackFrame.java, JDK 11](https://java-browser.yawk.at/java/11/java.base/java/lang/LiveStackFrame.java)); the public `StackWalker.StackFrame` exposes class, method, bytecode index and descriptor but no receiver or locals ([StackWalker javadoc](https://cr.openjdk.org/~alanb/sv-20240517/java.base/java/lang/StackWalker.html)). A JDK fork can own this directly; anything outside one needs `--patch-module` or JVMTI. Second, every deserialization defence located (JEP 290 filters, CERT SER12-J, OWASP guidance, the Seneca call-graph analysis) acts before or during construction; JEP 411 itself says only that "serialization filtering can prevent malicious data from being deserialized before it can do any damage" ([JEP 411](https://openjdk.org/jeps/411); [CERT SER12-J](https://wiki.sei.cmu.edu/confluence/spaces/java/pages/88487384/SER12-J.+Prevent+deserialization+of+untrusted+data); [arXiv 2311.00943](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.00943)). The closest in-the-wild practice found is Firmstone's own 2021 jdk-dev description of using "a ProtectionDomain to represent a remote server" and injecting a client Subject's permissions into the stack, which attaches the remote principal to the Subject and the class loader rather than to the deserialized objects and does not propose linking object provenance to the access-control context ([jdk-dev 2021-May/005465](https://mail.openjdk.org/pipermail/jdk-dev/2021-May/005465.html)); the Jini/River/JGDMS literature itself was not searched, so any in-project precedent must be checked locally.

## Canonical digest identities: hashing is ubiquitous, canonical input is rare, schema gating is unprecedented

For mechanism (3), the record splits into three findings. Digest-keyed authorization is everywhere, but almost nowhere is the digest input canonicalized. Schema fingerprints exist in several ecosystems and every one disclaims security. And every canonical form that has been hashed or signed in production has produced a specific bug class whose remedy is uniform. **No production system was found that refuses to decode on the basis of an untrusted schema digest as a security policy**, so Atomic DER's use of a schema digest as a gate appears novel in that respect; the searches that did not run (Protobuf descriptor hashing, Arrow Flight schema fingerprinting, ASN.1 module OIDs for gating) are listed in the gaps.

**Production digest-keyed policy hashes shipped bytes, pairs the hash with a rotatable identity, and resolves names to digests before evaluating policy.** Jini's `httpmd:` scheme (Sun "Davis," 2002–03) carried a message digest of the JAR in the codebase URL so that "HTTPMD URLs are a cheaper mechanism than HTTPS URLs" needing no server authentication, but the digest is over the raw JAR bytes as served and the policy key remains the codebase URL; Scheifler's reason for not sending code in-band was that doing so "makes it unclear which URL a class came from (which matters for security policies)" ([Artima, Scheifler 2002](https://www.artima.com/intv/objinteg.html); [HttpmdUtil](https://resources.gigaspaces.com/api/17.3.0/java/net/jini/url/httpmd/HttpmdUtil.html)). Nix store paths hash a `fingerprint` that deliberately includes the store directory so the same content at a different root is a different identity, and for sources hash "the NAR serialization of the file system object," a canonical archive rather than raw tar ([Nix store-path spec](https://nix.dev/manual/nix/2.28/protocols/store-path)). Kyverno's image verification mutates tag references to digests before signature verification because "Using an image digest has the benefit of making image references immutable and prevents spoofing attacks," then requires the digest and a verified annotation to be present, and documents the cost of caching: a verified image "will be considered to be verified until TTL duration expires" (defaults `imageVerifyCacheTTLDuration=60m`, `imageVerifyCacheMaxSize=1000`), so signature revocation lags by up to the TTL ([Kyverno](https://kyverno.io/docs/policy-types/cluster-policy/verify-images/)). Gradle hashes "the downloaded bytes" of every artifact including POMs, states that "Checksums alone verify integrity but not authenticity. Signatures alone verify authenticity but may use weak hashes," warns that bootstrapping "trusts whatever is currently in your repositories," and supports `also-trust` for multiple accepted digests per artifact ([Gradle](https://docs.gradle.org/current/userguide/dependency_verification.html)). Android grants signature-level permissions by digest of the signing certificate, and since Android 12 `knownCerts` matches "any signer in the requesting app's signing lineage, including the current signer," so key rotation preserves grants ([Android](https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/permissions/defining)); this is the closest analogue to "grant keyed on a DER digest," and DER's distinguished encoding is what makes the certificate digest stable. AppLocker states the maintenance cost of bare-hash rules plainly: "Each time that the file is updated (such as a security update or upgrade), the file's Authenticode hash changes. As a result, you must manually update file hash rules," while noting that the Authenticode hash is itself a canonicalized PE hash that skips the checksum field and signature directory, a rare canonicalized policy input ([Microsoft Learn, AppLocker](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/security/application-security/application-control/app-control-for-business/applocker/understanding-the-file-hash-rule-condition-in-applocker)). Linux IMA/EVM appraises file hashes in `security.ima` with an HMAC over metadata in `security.evm`, and "does not appraise the file name" ([IMA/EVM concepts](https://ima-doc.readthedocs.io/en/latest/ima-concepts.html)). The pattern across all of these is that a bare digest is un-rotatable and un-revocable without re-issue, so every system binds it to a second identity: Android's lineage, Gradle's signature, Kyverno and Sigstore's signer authority ([policy-controller API](https://github.com/sigstore/policy-controller/blob/main/docs/api-types/index-v1alpha1.md)), AppLocker's publisher rules. The only canonicalized inputs found, Authenticode and Nix NAR, canonicalize precisely so that incidental byte differences do not change identity, which is Atomic DER's reason for hashing a schema rather than class bytes.

**Schema fingerprints are lookup identifiers with explicit security disclaimers, and they differ on whether evolution changes identity.** Avro's Parsing Canonical Form strips attributes irrelevant to parsing (doc, aliases, defaults), fixes attribute order and whitespace, and is fingerprinted with Rabin-64, MD5 or SHA-256, with the single-object encoding prefixed by marker `C3 01` and the 8-byte CRC-64-AVRO fingerprint of the writer schema; the specification states that **"These fingerprints are not meant to provide any security guarantees, even the longer SHA-256-based ones"** ([Avro 1.11.1](https://avro.apache.org/docs/1.11.1/specification/)). Avro's canonical form has the same intent as a shape digest, identifying the parse shape rather than the document, and Avro's experience shows that shape-digest identity forces a registry or alias mechanism to map old to new digests on evolution. Cap'n Proto type IDs are the opposite: derived from the parent scope's ID and the declaration name, with 63 effective bits, so that "names can change as long as the type ID / ordinal numbers stay the same" and adding fields does not change the ID; the documentation notes collisions "from misuse (e.g. copying an example without changing the ID) are much more likely" than random ones and makes no statement about security ([Cap'n Proto language reference](https://android.kernel.org/toolchain/capnproto/+/refs/heads/upstream-release-0.10.3/doc/language.md)). Confluent and Apicurio wire formats carry a registry-assigned ID rather than a content hash, so the registry is the trust root and the identity is not self-certifying (titles only reached; [Apicurio](https://apicurio-serdes.readthedocs.io/en/latest/concepts/wire-format/)). A security-gating design therefore has to do what none of these do: use a collision-resistant digest, define the canonical form normatively with a versioned profile, and decide explicitly whether an added optional field yields a new identity (Avro yes, Cap'n Proto no). A schema digest that excludes method bodies is also a grant to "anything with this shape," closer to Cap'n Proto type-ID semantics than to a code-integrity check; integrity needs the bytes digest, stable policy identity needs the schema digest, and Android's lineage model is the precedent for binding the stable identity to a rotatable integrity key.

**Every hashed canonical form has produced a "two encodings, one value" bug, and the fix has always been the same.** In CMS, the practitioner-level confusion is that "DER is a legal encoding when BER is called for" while the signed attributes must be DER for the digest, as Schaad explained on the IETF S/MIME list in 2003 when a recipient saw a DER `31 82` SET where BER `30 80` was expected ([IETF S/MIME archive](https://mailarchive.ietf.org/arch/msg/smime/CbL_nglfi5Z1smxfviqNxHJ0Twk/)); the researcher's further claim that RFC 5652 §5.4 hashes a re-encoded DER form with a substituted tag was quoted from memory and not fetched, so it is flagged rather than relied on. Bitcoin's BIP 66 (2015) is the cleanest precedent for "gate on strict DER before anything else": because OpenSSL "is not designed for consensus-critical behaviour" and older versions accepted non-DER signatures, an OpenSSL update "made some nodes reject the chain"; the fix requires signatures to "be encoded using strict DER encoding," runs the encoding check before ECDSA verification, and on failure "the entire script evaluates to false immediately," with the side benefit of "reducing transaction malleability" ([BIP 66](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bitcoin/bips/master/bip-0066.mediawiki)). XML Signature's failure history is almost entirely about indirection between what was hashed and what is processed: Hill's 2007 iSEC analysis shows signed references resolved "independent of context" so signed elements can be swapped, C14N running before any cryptographic check and forcing entity expansion (about 2 GB from a recursive DTD) so it is reachable by anonymous attackers, injectable transforms with XSLT "totally unsuitable" because Xalan extensions reach command execution, and external references creating TOCTOU ([iSEC handout](https://nccgroup.com/media/ueejlzbz/_isec_hill_attackingxmlsecurity_handout.pdf)). Deterministic CBOR (RFC 8949, December 2020) mandates shortest-form integers and floats, forbids indefinite lengths, sorts map keys bytewise, and in §4.2.3 admits that this ordering differs from RFC 7049's "Canonical CBOR," so a digest of "canonical CBOR" is not stable across spec versions unless the profile is pinned ([RFC 8949](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8949.html)). JSON Canonicalization (RFC 8785, June 2020) requires I-JSON input with no duplicate names, aborts on any failure, and carries verified erratum EID 7920: because `-0` serializes as `0`, a parser "SHOULD generate an error condition" on `-0` "to guard against attacks on data not yet parsed," a two-encodings bug in the canonicalization specification itself ([RFC 8785 with errata](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/inline-errata/rfc8785.html)). Three design rules follow directly. The bytes hashed must be the bytes parsed, with no CMS-style re-encoding step where tag substitution and BER/DER bugs hide. A schema digest that identifies a type rather than an instance introduces XML-style indirection between the gated schema and the decoded instance, so the instance decoder must be strictly derived from the gated schema with no attacker-influenced transform. And the canonical-profile version must be part of the identity or carried alongside the digest, because canonical forms themselves drift.

Two further findings bear on the design's surrounding choices. On compression, the post-2012 consensus is never to place attacker-influenced data and secrets in one compression context: BREACH's authors name separating secrets from user input into "a completely different compression context" as the only mitigation that "completely solves the problem" ([BREACH 2013](https://css.csail.mit.edu/6.566/2014/readings/breach.pdf)); TLS 1.3 lists "removal of compression" among its major changes and requires `legacy_compression_methods` to be exactly one zero byte ([RFC 8446](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8446.html)); and HPACK replaced DEFLATE after CRIME with a public static table plus a per-connection dynamic table and never-indexed literals, with the advice that "An ideal solution segregates access to the dynamic table based on the entity that is constructing header fields" ([RFC 7541](https://rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7541)). "Compress and deduplicate the public schema but not the data" satisfies BREACH's rule by construction and matches HPACK's static-table architecture, but no document was found naming it as a pattern, so HPACK is the citation; the residual is that a shared dynamic schema dictionary across mutually distrustful peers reintroduces the problem HPACK §7.1.2 segregates against. On the loader-binding problem, the researcher cites but did not fetch the JVM specification's rule that a runtime class is identified by its binary name and defining loader ([JVMS SE 21 §5.3](https://docs.oracle.com/javase/specs/jvms/se21/html/jvms-5.html#jvms-5.3), wording unverified); the consequence, that a grant keyed only on `digest(classBytes)` is satisfied by every loader's copy including an attacker-created loader that mixes trusted bytes with hostile ones in one `ProtectionDomain`, means a schema digest that gates reconstruction *before* any class exists avoids the problem for the gate but any privilege granted afterward still lands on a loader-specific domain. Jini's resolution, using the digest for integrity of the source and letting the loader-scoped `CodeSource` carry the grant, is the available precedent; a digest-keyed grant should name the loading context or be applied by synthesising the `CodeSource`, not by matching bare bytes at check time. For agility, the observed strategies are to carry the algorithm name with the digest (Jini `;<algorithm>=`, Nix `fixed:out:<algo>:`, Gradle per-algorithm elements, Sigstore `hashAlgorithm`), to allow multiple digests per object (Gradle `also-trust`), and to bind the digest to a rotatable signer for revocation; revoking a bare digest grant is universally "remove the rule and redeploy."

## Conclusion

The three mechanisms sit in a gap that the literature walked around rather than through. The stack-inspection lineage (Wallach, JEP 140, OSGi, River, Elasticsearch) kept authority a property of code and either loosened elevation or abolished intersection; the information-flow lineage (HBAC, IBAC, Trishul, Laminar, Phosphor, Jif) made data provenance first-class but replaced stack inspection wholesale, paying 52–167% for propagation in research JVMs and never reaching a commodity runtime; and the digest-identity lineage (Jini httpmd, Nix, Kyverno, Gradle, Android, AppLocker) made hashes policy keys but hashed shipped bytes and leaned on signers for rotation, while the schema-fingerprint community (Avro, Cap'n Proto, Confluent) explicitly declined to make its digests security-relevant. Nobody combined per-class bounded elevation with recorded policy, nobody read receivers during a security walk on a stock JDK, and nobody gated decoding on a canonical schema digest. The JEP 411/486 decision to remove enforcement rather than enrich the walk also removed OpenJDK's incentive to explore any of this, which is why the gap is structural rather than evidence that the approaches were tried and failed.

Three consequences for the design note follow. The receiver-provenance walk cannot stop at `doPrivileged`: the .NET `Deny` precedent shows that a subtractive domain below a privileged frame is masked exactly where it matters, and IBAC's "always check the value's frame" is the precedent for continuing the collection, so the design should state that `getContext()` collects provenance domains across the full stack while authority domains still stop at the privileged frame, and should own that this is a semantic change to `doPrivileged`. The novelty claim for receiver provenance must cite Trishul for `thisTaint` and IBAC for permission-set labels through privileged blocks, and distinguish itself by boundary stamping, receiver collection and `ProtectionDomain` reuse rather than by the goal. And the digest-keyed grant should follow BIP 66's ordering (strict-DER gate before any cryptographic or policy step, on the very bytes parsed), carry the canonical-profile version with the digest, separate the schema digest (stable policy identity) from the bytes digest (integrity), and bind grants to a loader context or synthesised `CodeSource` rather than bare bytes, with ZTD_JAVA's DaCapo numbers as the benchmark the per-class variant has to match.
